## [MISSION BRIEF] Pixel's big business idea

Pixel has a business plan: "Pixel's Attention Scanner" will watch pupils' faces through classroom webcams and tell teachers who looks bored. It will be sold in schools across Europe, and Pixel has already printed the brochures. Dr. Ada Okafor reads one, puts it down gently and asks: "Pixel, have you checked whether this is even allowed?" Pixel has not. Nobody told Pixel that AI has rules.

### Learning goals

By the end of this mission you should be able to:

- **Explain** what artificial general intelligence (AGI) means, why there is no agreed definition or timeline, and summarize the main viewpoints fairly.
- **Describe** the risk-based approach of the **EU AI Act** and its four risk tiers, with examples.
- **Classify** AI systems into EU AI Act tiers, justify each choice, and **trace** which rules apply on a given date.
- **Compare** binding law (the EU AI Act) with non-binding principles (OECD, UNESCO).
- **Combine** everything from Missions 01–12 in a written review of an AI product (the capstone).

## [TRY IT] Activity 12.1: The risk-tier sorting desk

**Goal:** Classify AI systems into the four EU AI Act risk tiers with a step-by-step method, and notice that one system can fall under two tiers.

**Preparation:** Paper and pen. Draw four boxes labeled **Unacceptable (banned)**, **High risk**, **Transparency**, **Minimal**. About 25 minutes. No device needed.

**Dr. Ada's four questions, asked in order:**

1. **Is it a banned practice?** Banned (unacceptable-risk) practices include: social scoring; harmful manipulation or deception; exploiting people's vulnerabilities; predicting that an individual will commit a crime based only on profiling; untargeted scraping of internet or CCTV images to build face-recognition databases; **emotion recognition in workplaces and schools**; biometric categorization to infer sensitive traits; real-time remote biometric identification in public spaces for law enforcement (with narrow exceptions); and (added in 2026) AI that generates non-consensual intimate images or child sexual abuse material. -> If yes: **Unacceptable**.
2. **Is it used in a high-risk area?** Education (for example, exam scoring), employment (for example, CV sorting), access to essential services (for example, credit scoring), law enforcement, migration and border control, justice and democratic processes, critical infrastructure, **or** a safety component of a regulated product (for example, a medical device). -> If yes: **High risk**.
3. **Does it interact directly with people, or generate synthetic content** (text, images, audio, video)? -> If yes: **Transparency** duties (these can apply **in addition** to high-risk rules).
4. **None of the above?** -> **Minimal** risk: no specific new obligations.

**Debate (example):**
- *For:* "Capabilities have improved very fast as models, data and compute grew; few people in 2015 predicted how fluent chatbots would be by 2023. In the 2024 survey, researchers' 50% date moved 13 years earlier than in the same survey one year before. If this trend continues, general ability within ten years is possible."

## [EXPLAIN] The future: general AI, rules and you

### Thinking about the future carefully

Predictions about AI have a mixed record: researchers in the 1950s–60s expected human-level AI within a generation (then came two winters), while few people in 2015 predicted how fluent chatbots would be by 2023. Two habits help: **separate evidence from prediction** ("this system scored X on this test" vs "this means human-level AI by 2030"), and **notice who is speaking and why** (companies raising money, researchers, journalists and campaigners have different incentives).

### Artificial general intelligence (AGI)

**AGI** is a **hypothetical** AI that could learn and perform a very wide range of intellectual tasks at least as well as a skilled human, rather than one narrow task. **There is no single agreed definition**: some definitions focus on matching humans at most or every task, some on learning new skills as efficiently as a person, and some include physical abilities. In 2023 Google DeepMind researchers (Morris and colleagues) proposed "Levels of AGI" (from "Emerging" through "Competent", "Expert" and "Virtuoso" to "Superhuman"); under their scheme, 2023's chatbots counted only as "Emerging AGI". It is one proposal, not an official standard.

**Are today's chatbots AGI?** As in Mission 07: the **mainstream view is that current LLMs are not AGI**. This is debated, and there is **no agreed test or definition** that would settle it.

**Forecasts.** The largest survey of AI researchers so far (Grace and colleagues, published 2024) asked **2,778** researchers. Their combined forecast gave a **10% chance** that machines could outperform humans at every task by **2027** and a **50% chance by 2047**; the 2047 estimate was **13 years earlier** than in the same survey one year before; full automation of all human occupations got a 50% chance only by **2116**; and individual answers **disagreed very widely**. These are **opinions about the future**, not measurements.

| Viewpoint | Core argument | What would change their mind? |
|---|---|---|
| **"AGI could arrive soon"** | Capabilities have improved fast as models, data and compute grew | Progress stalling despite more data and compute |
| **"Current methods are not enough"** | Models still hallucinate, are brittle and need far more data than humans; new ideas are needed | Models overcoming these limits with current methods |
| **"Prepare for serious risks"** | Very capable AI could be misused or behave in unintended ways; build safety and oversight now | Strong evidence such systems can be reliably controlled |
| **"Focus on today's harms"** | AGI talk can distract from bias, privacy, misinformation, jobs and energy use | Clear, measurable definitions of AGI and evidence of its approach |

Many people hold parts of several views. A fair summary in 2026: **no consensus on what AGI is, whether current methods can reach it, or when it might arrive.** What is not in doubt is that today's narrow AI already raises real issues.

### Why regulate AI?

To protect **safety and fundamental rights**, make clear **who is accountable**, require **transparency**, and build **trust** so beneficial AI can be adopted, while not stifling useful innovation (a constant balancing act).

> **Playful analogy:** the EU AI Act works like the Curiosity Lab's safety rules. A few experiments are banned outright. Risky ones are allowed only with goggles, a supervisor and a logbook. Some just need a warning sign on the door. Most, like sorting paper clips, need no special rules at all.
>
> **Technically speaking:** this is a **risk-based approach**: obligations scale with the potential harm of a **use**, not with the technology inside. A provider (who develops and markets a system) and a deployer (an organization that uses it) have duties that depend on which tier the use falls into.

### The EU AI Act

The **EU AI Act** (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689) is the world's first comprehensive law on AI. It was adopted on 13 June 2024, published on 12 July 2024 and **entered into force on 1 August 2024**. It applies to organizations that place AI systems on the EU market or use them in the EU, **even if they are based outside the EU**.

| Tier | What happens | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Unacceptable risk** | **Banned** | Social scoring; harmful manipulation; exploiting vulnerabilities; crime prediction based only on profiling; untargeted face scraping; emotion recognition in workplaces and schools; biometric categorization of sensitive traits; real-time remote biometric identification in public spaces for law enforcement (narrow exceptions); (added 2026) generating non-consensual intimate images or child sexual abuse material |
| **High risk** | **Allowed with strict obligations**: risk management, high-quality data, documentation, logging, human oversight, accuracy and cybersecurity, registration | Education (exam scoring), employment (CV sorting), essential services (credit scoring), law enforcement, migration and border control, justice and democratic processes, critical infrastructure; safety components of regulated products (medical devices) |
| **Transparency risk** (often called "limited risk") | **Disclosure duties** | Chatbots must make clear you are talking to an AI; deepfakes and certain AI-generated content must be labeled |
| **Minimal or no risk** | **No specific new obligations** | Spam filters; AI in video games |

**General-purpose AI (GPAI) models**, such as the large LLMs behind popular chatbots, have **separate** rules for their providers (technical documentation, information for developers who build on them, and extra duties for the most powerful models). **Penalties:** breaking the bans can cost up to **€35 million or 7%** of worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher; most other breaches up to €15 million or 3%.

**The timeline.** In 2026 the EU amended the schedule through the **Digital Omnibus on AI** (proposed by the European Commission on 19 November 2025, politically agreed in May 2026, in force from 27 July 2026, according to the Commission):

| Date | What applies |
|---|---|
| 1 Aug 2024 | Act enters into force (no obligations yet) |
| 2 Feb 2025 | **Bans** on unacceptable-risk practices; AI literacy provisions |
| 2 Aug 2025 | Rules for **general-purpose AI models**; governance (national authorities, EU AI Office) |
| 2 Aug 2026 | Act **generally applies**, including **transparency** duties (chatbot disclosure, deepfake labeling) |
| 2 Dec 2026 | New ban on AI generating non-consensual intimate content or child sexual abuse material; end of a grace period for marking AI-generated content for systems already on the market before 2 Aug 2026 (added/changed by the Omnibus) |
| 2 Dec 2027 | **High-risk** obligations for stand-alone high-risk uses (education, employment, credit, etc.); **originally 2 Aug 2026**, delayed by the Omnibus |
| 2 Aug 2028 | High-risk obligations for AI built into regulated products (e.g. medical devices, machinery); **originally 2 Aug 2027**, delayed by the Omnibus |

**As of today (6 November 2026):** the bans, the GPAI rules and the transparency duties already apply; the main high-risk obligations do **not yet** apply.

> **Uncertainty note:** the Omnibus changes are recent, and secondary sources differ slightly on some details (for example, whether political agreement came on 6 or 7 May 2026). According to law-firm summaries, the Omnibus also softened the AI literacy duty, emphasizing promotion of AI literacy by the Commission and Member States rather than a strict duty on every company. For legal decisions, always check the current consolidated text on EUR-Lex.

### International principles: OECD and UNESCO

- **OECD AI Principles** (2019, updated May 2024): the first intergovernmental standard on AI, with 47 adherents by 2026. Five values-based principles (inclusive growth and well-being; human rights and democratic values including fairness and privacy; transparency and explainability; robustness, security and safety; accountability) and five recommendations for governments.
- **UNESCO Recommendation on the Ethics of AI** (November 2021): adopted by all 193 UNESCO member states; four core values and ten principles, including human oversight, transparency, fairness and non-discrimination, and awareness and literacy.
- **Binding vs non-binding:** the EU AI Act can **fine** companies. The OECD and UNESCO frameworks are **soft law**: they guide governments but cannot fine anyone. The Council of Europe's **Framework Convention on AI** (opened for signature in September 2024) is described as the first legally binding international treaty on AI and human rights.

### What can you do? An AI literacy checklist

1. **Verify before you trust** important outputs (Mission 11's four-step check).
2. **Match trust to stakes** (Mission 09).
3. **Protect privacy**: don't share personal, other people's or confidential information with AI tools unless you know how it will be used (Mission 10).
4. **Be open about AI use**, following your school's or workplace's rules.
5. **Watch for deepfakes and manipulation**: confirm surprising requests through a second channel.
6. **Ask the governance questions**: Who built this? What data does it use? Who is accountable if it's wrong? Can I ask a human?
7. **Keep learning.** The concepts in this guide (data, training, inference, bias, hallucination, risk) will help you understand whatever comes next.

For more depth, including a full worked classification, read [Topic 10](../../task-1/topics/10-future-of-ai.md).

## [LAB NOTE] What this mission simplifies

- The sorting desk uses short card descriptions. Real legal classification depends on detailed definitions, exceptions and the exact use; this mission teaches the **logic**, not legal advice.
- The EU AI Act is one law for one region. Other countries take different approaches, and laws change (as the 2026 Omnibus shows).
- The debate cards compress real research into one sentence each. Forecast surveys measure **opinions**; they cannot measure the future.

## Mission summary

- **AGI** is hypothetical with **no agreed definition**; the mainstream view is that current LLMs are **not AGI** (debated, no agreed test). Researcher forecasts (2024 survey: 50% by 2047) are opinions with wide disagreement.
- The **EU AI Act** (in force 1 Aug 2024) sorts AI **uses** into four tiers: **unacceptable** (banned), **high** (strict obligations), **transparency** (disclosure) and **minimal** (no new obligations). One system can fall under two tiers.
- Rules apply **in stages**: bans 2 Feb 2025; GPAI 2 Aug 2025; transparency 2 Aug 2026; new ban 2 Dec 2026; high-risk 2 Dec 2027 and 2 Aug 2028 (after the 2026 Digital Omnibus delay).
- **OECD** (2019, updated 2024) and **UNESCO** (2021) principles are non-binding guidance; the EU AI Act is binding law with fines.
- **You** shape AI's future through AI literacy: verify, match trust to stakes, protect privacy, disclose AI use, watch for deepfakes, ask governance questions, keep learning.

> **[MISSION BRIEF]** A company has asked the Curiosity Lab to review its new product before it launches in European schools. Dr. Ada Okafor hands you the file: "Pixel has been promoted to assistant. You are the reviewer. One page. Everything you have learned." Pixel straightens its detective hat, then remembers it is no longer a detective, then puts on the judge's wig, then takes that off too.

**Goal:** Write a one-page **AI system review** of a fictional product, using the tools from all twelve missions: what AI is and what kind (Missions 01–07), the hype pattern (Mission 08), the six-part framework (Mission 09), the bias life cycle and six issues (Mission 10), the misconceptions and four-step checks (Missions 06 and 11), and the EU AI Act tiers and dates (Mission 12).

**Preparation:** This guide, your notes from earlier missions, and about 60–90 minutes. No device needed. *MarkMate, EduGenius Ltd, the "Institute for Learning Futures" and all figures below are fictional, made up for this challenge; use only the facts given.*

### The product file: MarkMate

> **MarkMate** by EduGenius Ltd. Planned launch: **September 2027**, in secondary schools across the **EU**. Three features:
>
> 1. **AutoGrade.** Scores students' **final-exam essays** from 0 to 100. A score below 50 means the student **fails**. Teachers can see the scores, but the plan is that they will "only check a score if a student complains".
> 2. **Ask Mia.** A chat assistant built on a large language model. Students ask it questions about their feedback. It signs its messages "Mia, your study coach" and **does not say it is an AI**.
> 3. **FocusCam** (optional add-on). Uses the classroom webcam to read pupils' **facial expressions** and reports to the teacher who is "bored" or "engaged".
>
> **Data and testing facts:**
> - (a) AutoGrade was trained on **2 million essays** written **2010–2020**, in English, by students at schools in **one country**. Each essay is labeled with the grade a teacher gave it.
> - (b) Vendor test: **94%** of AutoGrade's scores were within 5 points of a teacher's grade on a held-back test set from **the same schools**. No results are broken down by group (for example, students writing in a second language, or students with dyslexia).
> - (c) All essays and all chats with Mia are stored on EduGenius's servers for **5 years** and used to train future versions.
> - (d) In a pilot, Mia once recommended a textbook **that does not exist**.
>
> **Marketing claims:**
> - **M1** "Mia understands every student like a human teacher would."
> - **M2** "AutoGrade is 100% objective: no human bias."
> - **M3** "Trained on 2 million essays, so it works for every student."
> - **M4** "A 2024 study by the Institute for Learning Futures proves MarkMate saves teachers 10 hours per week." (No link or reference is given.)
> - **M5** "Within five years, MarkMate will replace teachers entirely."

### Your task: the one-page review

Write your review under these **six headings** (aim for 500–800 words; bullet points are fine):

1. **What is it?** For each feature: input -> output, the kind of AI (rule-based, machine learning, deep learning, generative) and its type under **both lenses** from Mission 07.
2. **Six-part analysis of AutoGrade** (input, output, AI type, benefit, risk, human oversight), with a **stakes** rating.
3. **Bias life-cycle check.** Identify at least **three** stages where bias could enter, each linked to a fact (a–d).
4. **Claims check.** Classify each marketing claim (M1–M5): which misconception or warning sign is it? Apply the **four-step check** to M4. Explain what fact (d) tells you about Mia.
5. **EU AI Act.** Give the tier for **each feature**, the deciding reason, and the date from which the obligations apply. Comment on the launch date.
6. **Recommendation.** Approve as is / approve with conditions / do not approve. Justify it in 3–4 sentences and list **at least five** specific safeguards, including who is accountable.

**Observation:** Before writing your recommendation, list the three facts that worried you most.

**Reflection:** After you finish, check your review against the rubric below and give yourself a score. Which mission's ideas did you use most? Which did you forget?

<summary>[DEBRIEF] Model review: MarkMate</summary>

**AI System Review: MarkMate (EduGenius Ltd)** · Reviewer: Curiosity Lab · Date: 6 November 2026

**1. What is it?**
- **AutoGrade:** essay text -> score 0–100 and pass/fail. **Machine learning** (supervised; probably deep learning, as it processes text), trained on essays labeled with teachers' grades. Narrow AI; **reactive** (judges each essay on its own).
- **Ask Mia:** student question -> generated text answer. **Generative AI** (an LLM, which is deep learning). Narrow AI (mainstream view); **limited memory** (uses the conversation so far, within its context window).
- **FocusCam:** webcam video -> "bored"/"engaged" labels. **Deep learning** (computer vision). Narrow AI; reactive or limited memory; **not theory of mind**: labeling expressions is not understanding feelings.

**2. Six-part analysis of AutoGrade**
| Input | Output | AI type | Benefit | Risk | Human oversight |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Exam essays | Score and pass/fail | Supervised ML | Faster marking, more consistent scoring, quicker feedback | Unfair or wrong fails; bias against groups unlike the training data; over-trust by teachers | Currently almost none ("only if a student complains"), which is far too little |

**Stakes: high.** A wrong fail can change a student's future, and students cannot see how the score was produced. High stakes require strong human oversight.

**3. Bias life-cycle check**
- **Problem definition / labeling (a):** the target is "the grade a teacher gave" in 2010–2020, a **proxy** for essay quality. Any bias in those teachers' grading becomes the "right answer".
- **Data collection (a):** one country, one language, one decade. Students in other EU countries, other curricula, or writing in a second language are under-represented.
- **Testing (b):** tested only on the **same schools**, with no breakdown by group. A high average (94%) can hide poor results for some groups, as in Gender Shades.
- **Deployment (a, b):** used EU-wide on students unlike the training data (**distribution shift**).
- **Feedback loop (c):** new essays and chats train future versions, so errors and biases can grow.

**4. Claims check**
- **M1:** misconception, "AI understands like humans". Mia generates likely text; it does not understand students. Theory of mind is hypothetical.
- **M2:** misconception, "AI is always objective". It copies teachers' past grades, including any bias, consistently and at scale.
- **M3:** misconception, "more data always fixes it", plus brittleness. Two million essays from one country are not representative of every student.
- **M4 (four-step check):** *Split:* a 2024 study exists; by the "Institute for Learning Futures"; it "proves"; 10 hours per week. *Prioritize:* the named source and the exact number. *Verify:* we need the study itself: does it exist, who funded it, how was "10 hours" measured, and does it say "proves"? No reference is given, so it cannot be verified. *Decide:* treat it as **unverified** and ask EduGenius for the full study before relying on it.
- **M5:** misconception, "AI will take all jobs", and a **hype warning sign** (an over-generalized promise, Mission 08). AI automates tasks, such as some marking, not the whole job of teaching.
- **Fact (d):** Mia **hallucinated** a textbook. It produces likely text, not checked facts. Students may waste time or trust false information; Mia needs grounding in approved materials and clear warnings.

**5. EU AI Act**
- **FocusCam: Unacceptable (banned).** Emotion recognition in education institutions has been prohibited since **2 February 2025**. It cannot be offered in EU schools at all.
- **AutoGrade: High risk** (education; it decides exam results). Obligations (risk management, high-quality data, documentation, logging, **human oversight**, accuracy, registration) apply from **2 December 2027** after the 2026 Omnibus delay (originally 2 August 2026). The September 2027 launch is about three months earlier, but the obligations will apply soon after, and a responsible provider should meet them **at launch**.
- **Ask Mia: Transparency.** It interacts directly with students, who must be told they are talking to an AI; this duty has applied since **2 August 2026**. "Mia, your study coach" without disclosure would break it. (The provider of the underlying general-purpose model also has its own GPAI obligations.)

**6. Recommendation: do not approve as is; approve only with conditions.**
MarkMate offers real benefits (faster marking and feedback), but in its current form one feature is illegal, one breaks a transparency duty that already applies, and the most consequential feature makes high-stakes decisions with almost no human oversight, on data and tests that cannot show it is fair to all students. Its marketing makes misleading claims. With the conditions below, AutoGrade and Ask Mia could be piloted as **assistants to teachers**, not replacements.
Safeguards:
1. **Remove FocusCam** from the EU product.
2. **A teacher reviews every fail** and a random sample of other scores; the teacher, not the software, makes the final decision. The **school** (deployer) is accountable for grades; **EduGenius** (provider) is accountable for the system's quality and documentation.
3. **Test results separately by group** (second-language writers, students with dyslexia, each country and curriculum) before launch in each country; publish the results.
4. **Mia must say it is an AI**, answer from approved course materials (grounding), and warn students to check references.
5. **Data minimization:** shorter storage, and no training on students' chats or essays without clear consent; comply with data-protection law.
6. Students can **see the reasons** for a score and **appeal** to a human; keep logs.
7. **Withdraw claims M1, M2, M3 and M5**, and publish the M4 study or remove the claim.
