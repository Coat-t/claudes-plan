## [MISSION BRIEF] Pixel's very long ladder

The Curiosity Lab is opening a small museum, and Pixel has been asked to sort the exhibit labels by "type of AI". Pixel proudly lines them up in one long row, like the rungs of a ladder: *reactive, narrow, limited memory, general, theory of mind, superintelligent, self-aware*. Dr. Ada Okafor looks at the row for a long time. "Pixel, you have answered two different questions at once. Let's untangle them."

### Learning goals

By the end of this mission you should be able to:

- **Explain** the two separate ways of classifying AI: **by capability** (narrow, general, superintelligent) and **by functionality** (reactive machines, limited memory, theory of mind, self-aware).
- **Distinguish** which types exist today and which are hypothetical.
- **Classify** a real or imagined system under **both** lenses and **justify** each label.
- **Correct** misleading claims such as "chatbots are general AI" or "this app understands your emotions".

## [TRY IT] Activity 7.1: Size and fuel (warm-up, 5 minutes)

**Goal:** Feel the difference between "one list" and "two separate ways of sorting" before you meet the AI version.

**Preparation:** Paper and pen. Nothing else.

**Task:** Here are six vehicles: a small electric car, a large diesel truck, a medium gasoline van, a small gasoline scooter, a large electric bus, a medium diesel tractor.

1. Sort them by **size** (small, medium, large).
2. Now sort the same six by **fuel** (gasoline, diesel, electric).
3. Pixel says: "So the order is small, gasoline, medium, diesel, large, electric." What went wrong?

**Observation:** Did any vehicle appear in both of your sorts? Did any vehicle need *two* labels to be fully described?

**Reflection:** If someone asks "Is the bus large or electric?", what is the right answer?

**Feedback:**

<details>
<summary>[DEBRIEF] Warm-up answers</summary>

Every vehicle appears in **both** sorts and needs **two** labels (for example, the bus is *large* **and** *electric*). Size and fuel answer different questions, so "large or electric?" is a trick question: the answer is "both". Pixel's mistake was to mix two separate sorting questions into one ladder. That is exactly what happens with the two common lists of "types of AI", which you will untangle next.
</details>

## [TRY IT] Activity 7.2: The two-lens card sort

**Goal:** Classify AI systems with two separate lenses, discover that every real system lands in only a few places, and practice justifying a label.

**Preparation:** Paper and pen (or sticky notes). Copy the **sorting board** below onto a large sheet: 3 rows x 4 columns, plus a strip called **"Can't decide from the description"**. Write each card number on a scrap of paper, or just write the numbers into the board. Works alone, in pairs or as a class. About 20 minutes. No device needed.

**The two questions (Dr. Ada's whiteboard):**

- **Lens 1, capability: How broad and powerful is it, compared with humans?**
  - *Narrow AI (ANI)*: built for one task or a limited range of tasks.
  - *General AI (AGI)*: would match humans across essentially any intellectual task (hypothetical).
  - *Superintelligence (ASI)*: would greatly exceed the best humans in virtually every area (speculative).
- **Lens 2, functionality: How does it use memory, and does it understand others or itself?**
  - *Reactive machine*: responds only to the current input; no memory of earlier moments.
  - *Limited memory*: uses information from the **recent past while it is working** (the last few seconds of sensor data, the conversation so far).
  - *Theory of mind*: would genuinely understand that people have their own thoughts, feelings and intentions (hypothetical).
  - *Self-aware*: would have awareness of its own internal states (hypothetical).

## [EXPLAIN] Two lenses, not one ladder

### Why there are "two lists"

Search online for "types of AI" and you will usually find two different lists mixed together. They answer **different questions**:

| | Lens 1: by capability | Lens 2: by functionality |
|---|---|---|
| Question asked | How broad and powerful is the AI's ability, compared with humans? | How does the AI use memory, and does it understand others or itself? |
| Categories | Narrow (ANI), General (AGI), Superintelligence (ASI) | Reactive machines, Limited memory, Theory of mind, Self-aware |
| Exist today | Narrow only | Reactive and limited memory only |
| Hypothetical | General, Superintelligence | Theory of mind, Self-aware |
| Status of the scheme | Widely used in research, policy and the media; AGI has no agreed definition | A popular-science taxonomy (Hintze, 2016), not a formal scientific standard |

> **Playful analogy:** the two lenses are like sorting vehicles by *size* and by *fuel*. A small car can be electric; a big truck can be diesel. Asking "is the bus large or electric?" makes no sense, and asking "is the chatbot narrow or limited memory?" makes no sense either. It is both.
>
> **Technically speaking:** a classification scheme (taxonomy) sorts things according to one chosen question. Lens 1 sorts AI by the *breadth* of its abilities; Lens 2 sorts it by how it uses *information about the recent past* and whether it *models minds*. IBM's explainer, for example, places ChatGPT under "narrow" in one scheme and under "limited memory" in the other.

### Lens 1: capability

- **Narrow AI (ANI)** is built for one task or a limited range of tasks: recognizing faces, filtering spam, playing chess, driving in particular conditions. It may be superhuman at its task but cannot transfer the skill: the chess program cannot drive a car. **All AI that exists today is narrow AI.** It is sometimes called "weak AI".
- **General AI (AGI)** is **hypothetical**: AI that could match (or exceed) human abilities across essentially any intellectual task, and learn new tasks as flexibly as a person. Experts do not agree on exactly what should count as AGI. It is sometimes called "strong AI".
- **Superintelligence (ASI)** is **speculative**: AI that would greatly exceed the best humans "in virtually all domains of interest" (philosopher Nick Bostrom, 2014). A chess program is better than any human *at chess*, but that does not make it superintelligent.

**What about chatbots?** Today's LLM chatbots are much broader than older narrow AI. But they work within limits: mostly text and images, frequent errors such as hallucinations (Mission 06), no reliable action in the physical world, and no learning from experience after training unless they are retrained. **The mainstream view is that current LLMs are not AGI.** Some researchers disagree, and there is **no agreed test** that would settle the question. Mission 12 returns to this debate.

### Lens 2: functionality

This scheme was made popular by a 2016 article by researcher Arend Hintze in *The Conversation* and is repeated by many industry explainers. It is useful, but it is **a popular-press taxonomy, not a formal scientific standard**: definitions vary and the boundaries are fuzzy (remember card 8).

- **Reactive machines** respond only to the current input. Example: Deep Blue (1997) looked at the current board and chose a move.
- **Limited memory** systems use information from the recent past *while they work*, but do not build up lasting life experience. Examples: a self-driving car tracking nearby cars; a chatbot using the conversation so far.
- **Theory of mind** (hypothetical) would genuinely model people's thoughts, feelings and intentions. "Emotion AI" can label facial expressions, but labeling is not understanding. A chatbot that writes "I understand how you feel" is producing likely text (Mission 06), not modeling your mind.
- **Self-aware AI** (hypothetical) would be aware of its own internal states. No existing system is accepted as self-aware; Mission 11 explains why "AI is conscious" is a misconception.

**The most common trap:** in this scheme, "memory" means **using the recent past while operating**. It does **not** mean training data. Almost every modern system was trained on past data; that alone does not make it "limited memory".

### Putting the lenses together

![Two side-by-side panels. Lens 1, by capability: narrow AI (exists today), general AI (hypothetical), superintelligence (speculative). Lens 2, by functionality: reactive machines and limited memory (exist today), theory of mind and self-aware AI (hypothetical).](../assets/images/types-of-ai-two-lenses.svg)

*Figure: Types of AI seen through two different lenses. The same system can be described with both. All AI that exists today is narrow, and works as a reactive or limited-memory system.* (Original diagram created for this module.)

**Key point:** all AI that exists today is **narrow AI**, working as **reactive** or **limited-memory** systems. The lenses also do not line up neatly: it is not agreed whether an AGI would need theory of mind or self-awareness, so you cannot convert "general" into a functionality type, or the other way round.

**Why this matters:** headlines such as "AI that thinks like a human" or "the first general AI" become easy to question. Ask: *Which task is it narrow at? Does it use the recent past while operating? Is there any evidence it understands people, or is it producing likely text?* That protects you from both hype ("it's basically a person") and dismissal ("it's just autocomplete, it can't do anything").

## [LAB NOTE] What this sorting game leaves out

- The card descriptions are simplified. Real products combine many parts (a self-driving car has dozens of models), and each part could be classified separately.
- The functionality lens is a teaching tool from popular science, not an official standard. Different sources may place the same product differently; **state your reason** and you are doing it right.
- Pixel is a fictional robot. Pixel would be narrow AI (it does a few lab jobs, badly) with limited memory (it remembers the last few things Dr. Ada said, most of the time).

## [APPLY] Activity 7.3: The headline fixer

**Goal:** Use both lenses to spot and correct misleading claims about AI.

**Preparation:** Your sorting board from Activity 7.2 and the [EXPLAIN] section. About 10 minutes.

**Task:** The Curiosity Lab's news feed has four headlines. For each one: (a) classify the system with **both** lenses, (b) say what is wrong with the headline, and (c) rewrite the headline so that it is accurate but still interesting.

- **A.** "Chess computer is SUPERINTELLIGENT, beats world champion!" *(The article describes a program that only plays chess.)*
- **B.** "New app understands your emotions!" *(The app looks at a photo and outputs "happy", "sad" or "angry".)*
- **C.** "This chatbot is limited-memory AI because it learned from the whole internet." *(The article is about an LLM chatbot.)*
- **D.** "First general AI is here: chatbot can talk about any topic!"

**Observation:** For each headline, note which lens the headline writer confused or misused.

**Reflection:** Which of the four errors do you think is most common in real news? Why might headline writers make it?

**Feedback:**

<details>
<summary>[DEBRIEF] Headline fixes</summary>

- **A.** Narrow + reactive. Superhuman at **one** task is not superintelligence, which means exceeding humans in *virtually all* areas. *Better:* "Chess program beats world champion at chess."
- **B.** Narrow + reactive (one photo at a time), **not** theory of mind. Labeling an expression is not understanding feelings. *Better:* "New app labels facial expressions in photos."
- **C.** The label "limited memory" can be right for a chatbot, but the **reason** is wrong: training data is not "memory" in this scheme. The chatbot counts as limited memory because it uses the **conversation so far, within its context window**, while it answers. *Better:* "Chatbot keeps track of your conversation, up to a limit."
- **D.** Under the mainstream view this is **narrow** AI: breadth of conversation is not human-level ability across every task. The question is debated and there is no agreed test, so "First general AI is here" states a contested claim as fact. *Better:* "New chatbot handles a wide range of topics; experts debate how general it really is."

**Reflection:** A and D (confusing "very good" or "very broad" with superintelligent or general) are probably the most common, because they make the most exciting headlines. Exciting claims spread; careful ones rarely do. Mission 08 shows how this kind of over-promising has caused trouble before.
</details>

## Mission summary

- There are **two separate classifications** of AI: by **capability** (narrow, general, superintelligent) and by **functionality** (reactive, limited memory, theory of mind, self-aware). They are different lenses, not one ladder.
- **Every system gets one label from each lens**, like a vehicle that is both "small" and "electric".
- **All AI that exists today is narrow**, and works as **reactive** or **limited memory**. AGI, ASI, theory of mind and self-awareness are hypothetical.
- In the functionality lens, **"memory" means using the recent past while operating**, not training data.
- The mainstream view is that current LLM chatbots are **narrow, not AGI**; this is debated and there is **no agreed test**. The functionality scheme is a popular-press taxonomy, so **state your reasons**.
