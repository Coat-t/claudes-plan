## How to use this test bank

**For learners (self-review)**

1. Finish a mission in the study guide first. Then answer that mission's questions **on paper, without looking back** at the guide.
2. Write a short reason even for True/False and Multiple Choice questions. Your reason tells you *why* you got something wrong.
3. Mark your answers with the **ANSWER KEY** at the end of this file. For every wrong answer, read the explanation, then review the topic section and mission named in the key.
4. Mixed review: before a final test, answer every fifth question across all missions (Q5, Q10, Q15, ...).

**For teachers**

- Question numbers are **global** (Q1-Q115) and never change, so you can build quizzes from any mix of questions and still use this key.
- Some missions start with an **information box** (for example, the puzzle rules or a Prolog program). If you copy a question into a quiz, copy its information box too. Apart from that, every question is self-contained: **no website, platform or program is needed** to answer it.
- **Suggested scoring:** True/False = 1 mark. Multiple Choice "Select ONE answer" = 1 mark. Multiple Choice "Select ALL that apply" = 2 marks (2 = exactly the correct options; 1 = one wrong or missing option; 0 = more than one). Open-ended questions show their marks; the key gives a sample answer and a marking guide. Accept other answers that are accurate and well justified.
- **Calculations** need only simple arithmetic; a basic calculator is allowed.
- **Activity links:** questions marked *(Activity: ...)* re-use the setting of an activity from the guide, but supply all the information needed.

**About the facts and numbers.** Real-world facts, dates and figures match the module's topic files (checked on 2026-11-06). Numbers marked **illustrative (made-up)** were invented for teaching. Characters and organizations such as Pixel, Dr. Ada Okafor, the Curiosity Lab, LoanLight or RoboChef are **fictional**.

## Mission 02: The River Problem

> **Information box for Q10 and Q12-Q17: the river-crossing puzzle**
> A farmer (F) must take a wolf (W), a goat (G) and a cabbage (C) from the left bank to the right bank of a river. The boat holds the farmer plus **at most one** other item, and only the farmer can row. If the farmer is not on the same bank, the **wolf eats the goat** and the **goat eats the cabbage**.
> A **state** is written `left bank | right bank`. Start: `FWGC | -`. Goal: `- | FWGC`. A state is **safe** if neither "W with G" nor "G with C" is left on a bank without F.

**Q9.** [Mission 02 · Topic 02 · TF]
Breadth-first search checks every state reachable in 1 move, then every state reachable in 2 moves, and so on, so the first solution it finds uses the fewest moves.

**Q10.** [Mission 02 · Topic 02 · TF]
In the state `C | FWG`, the farmer can safely row back to the left bank alone.

**Q11.** [Mission 02 · Topic 02 · TF]

**Q17.** [Mission 02 · Topic 02 · OE] *(5 marks)* *(Activity: watch Pixel search)*
Pixel solves the puzzle with breadth-first search (BFS). At each step it takes the state at the **front** of the frontier (a queue) and tries four options **in this order**: farmer alone, with the wolf, with the goat, with the cabbage. An option is **rejected** if the item is not on the farmer's bank or the new state is unsafe; it is **skipped** if the new state is already visited; otherwise the new state is **added to the back** of the frontier and to the visited list.
The first three steps are done for you:

| Step | State taken from the frontier | New states added | Frontier after the step |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | `FWGC \| -` | `WC \| FG` | `WC \| FG` |
| 2 | `WC \| FG` | `FWC \| G` | `FWC \| G` |
| 3 | `FWC \| G` | `C \| FWG`, `W \| FGC` | `C \| FWG`, `W \| FGC` |

Visited after step 3: `FWGC | -`, `WC | FG`, `FWC | G`, `C | FWG`, `W | FGC`.

a) Complete **steps 4 and 5**. For each step, list what happens to each of the four options (rejected, skipped or added) and give the frontier afterwards.
b) When BFS eventually reaches the goal, how many crossings does its plan contain? Why can no plan be shorter?

## Mission 03: Pixel's Rulebook

> **Information box for Q18-Q26: Prolog in brief**
> - A **fact** states something true and ends with a period: `parent(grace, ada).` means "grace is a parent of ada".
> - Names starting with a **lowercase** letter (`grace`, `ada`) are **atoms** (fixed names). Names starting with an **uppercase** letter (`X`, `Child`) are **variables** that Prolog tries to fill in.
> - A **rule** `head :- condition1, condition2.` means "head is true **IF** condition1 **AND** condition2".
> - `X \= Y` means "X and Y are different". `\+ Goal` means "Goal cannot be proved". `_` means "anything".
> - Prolog tries facts and rules **from top to bottom** and lists every way it can prove a query. `false` means "cannot be proved from this program".
>
> **The family program (used in Q19, Q21, Q22, Q24, Q25, Q26).** The family is made up.
> ```prolog
> parent(grace, ada).
> parent(samuel, ada).
> parent(grace, tunde).
> parent(samuel, tunde).
> parent(ada, zara).
> parent(ada, leo).
> parent(tunde, kemi).
>
> male(samuel).
> male(tunde).
> male(leo).
>
> female(grace).
> female(ada).
> female(zara).
> female(kemi).
>
> grandparent(X, Z) :- parent(X, Y), parent(Y, Z).
> sibling(X, Y)     :- parent(P, X), parent(P, Y), X \= Y.
> ancestor(X, Y)    :- parent(X, Y).
> ancestor(X, Y)    :- parent(X, Z), ancestor(Z, Y).
> ```

**Review:** [Topic 08: Ethics, Risks and Social Impact of AI](../../task-1/topics/08-ethics-risks-social-impact.md) · [Mission 10: The Fairness Files](../study-guide/mission-10-the-fairness-files.md)

**Q87. FALSE.**
*Corrected statement:* "Bias can enter at **every stage** of an AI system's life cycle: problem definition (a poor proxy target), data collection, labeling, training, testing (checking only overall accuracy), deployment (a different population) and feedback loops." (Topic 08, section 2 and Common mistake 2.)

**Q88. TRUE.** Buolamwini and Gebru tested three commercial gender-classification systems on a balanced set of 1,270 photos of parliamentarians; the worst error rate for lighter-skinned men was 0.8%, while darker-skinned women had error rates of up to 34.7%. (Topic 08, section 2.)

**Q89. FALSE.**
*Corrected statement:* "AI systems are not legal persons. The **people and organizations** that build, deploy and use them are responsible. In *Mata v. Avianca* (June 2023), lawyers who submitted six non-existent ChatGPT-generated cases were fined $5,000 in total." (Topic 08, section 5 and Common mistake 4.)

**Q90. B and D.**
- A and C (wrong): nothing in the two facts concerns energy use or fake media.
- B (correct): training on past investigations that targeted some neighborhoods more often teaches the model that pattern (**bias**).
- D (correct): nobody can explain a flag, so claimants cannot challenge it and responsibility is unclear (**accountability and transparency**).

**Q91. B (problem definition).** Choosing "future cost" as a stand-in (proxy) for "how sick" is a design choice about **what to predict**. In the real 2019 case (Obermeyer and colleagues), less money had historically been spent on Black patients with the same illness, so the algorithm under-identified them for extra care (by more than half); predicting health needs directly removed much of the bias.
- A (wrong): the problem was the choice of target, not human labelers' judgments.
- C and D (wrong): the bias was built in before deployment and before any feedback loop.

**Q92. A.** Under-represented classes are learned less well, and uncertain inputs often lean toward a better-represented class. This is a typical tendency, not a guaranteed rule (shortcuts such as the background can also matter), so the fix is to add **more varied** examples of the weak class, retrain and **test again**. This mirrors Gender Shades: groups that are under-represented in the data get worse results.
- B (wrong): the model has no idea of politeness; it only finds patterns in pixels.
- C (wrong): overfitting is not caused by having more data, and deleting data without retesting gives you no evidence.
- D (wrong): there is no such rule; class order does not decide the output.

**Q93. D.** Testing on a **balanced** set and reporting results **separately for each group** (disaggregated evaluation) is exactly what exposed the gap.
- A (wrong): a bigger test set with the same imbalance would hide the gap again.
- B and C (wrong): training longer or faster hardware does not reveal how errors are distributed across groups.

**Q94. Sample answer and marking guide (8 marks).**
a) Any three stages, each linked to a fact *(3 marks)*:
- **Problem definition / labeling:** the label "was approved" copies past officers' decisions, which treated some neighborhoods unequally (a fairer target might be "repaid the loan", though that also needs checking).
- **Data collection:** ten years of historical decisions carry past unfairness, and some groups may be under-represented.
- **Features / training:** postcode acts as a **proxy** for neighborhood (and possibly for income or ethnicity), so the model learns to penalize it, although nobody wrote "reject this neighborhood".
- **Testing:** only overall accuracy was checked; no results by group or neighborhood.
- **Deployment:** automatic rejection of the bottom 30% with no human review.
- **Feedback loop:** rejected applicants never get loans, so no repayment data is collected for them, and the bias can grow in the next training round.
b) 91% measures how well the model **copies past decisions**, including their unfairness, and an overall figure can hide large differences between groups. Evidence: Gender Shades, where overall accuracy hid error rates of up to 34.7% for darker-skinned women versus at most 0.8% for lighter-skinned men; or Amazon's experimental hiring tool, which learned to penalize "women's" from ten years of mostly male resumes. *(2 marks.)*
c) Any four *(2 marks)*: reconsider the label/target; remove or test proxy features such as postcode; measure approval rates and error rates **separately** for each group and neighborhood; human review of rejections; explanations and a right to appeal; independent audits; monitoring after deployment; do not deploy if the bias cannot be reduced to an acceptable level.
d) **High risk** (access to essential services: credit scoring). The bank and the provider must meet strict obligations such as risk management, high-quality representative data, documentation, logging, human oversight, accuracy and registration; after the 2026 Digital Omnibus these stand-alone high-risk obligations apply from **2 December 2027**. *(1 mark.)*

**Q95. Sample answer and marking guide (6 marks).** (Calculation verified.)
a) 45,000 x 0.2% = 45,000 x 0.002 = **90 false alerts per day**; 90 x 7 = **630 per week**: 630 innocent people stopped each week, even with a "tiny" error rate. *(2 marks.)*
b) Any three, each linked to a fact *(3 marks)*:
- **Bias:** tested mainly on lighter-skinned adults, so false matches may be more frequent for darker-skinned people and children, who would then be stopped more often (Gender Shades evidence).
- **Privacy:** every visitor is filmed and stored for 30 days, without being told or consenting.
- **Accountability and transparency:** visitors are not informed, so wrongful stops are hard to challenge, and it is unclear who is responsible (vendor, city or police).
c) Example: "Do not deploy as is. Even a small error rate means hundreds of wrongful stops a week, likely concentrated on groups the system was not tested on, and nobody is told they are being scanned. It should be considered only after independent testing by group, public notice, immediate deletion of non-matching images and a human check of every alert." Accept "deploy with strict conditions" if justified with the facts. (In the EU, real-time remote biometric identification in public spaces for law enforcement is banned except in narrow, legally authorized cases.) *(1 mark.)*

**Q96. Sample answer and marking guide (5 marks).** (Calculation verified.)
a) 170 million - 92 million = **78 million** (net). 92 ÷ 170 ≈ 0.54, so about **5 jobs displaced for every 10 created**. *(2 marks.)*
b) Any two *(2 marks)*: 78 million is a **net** figure, not "new jobs" (170 million created, 92 million displaced); the change is driven by **several trends** (technology including AI, the green transition, economic pressures, population changes), not AI alone; it is a **projection** from employer surveys, not a fact; "nobody needs to worry" ignores the 92 million displaced jobs.
c) The people who lose jobs are often not the people who get the new ones: new jobs may be in different industries, places or skill levels. Without retraining and support, displaced workers can face unemployment or lower pay even while the total grows. *(1 mark.)*

## Answers: Mission 11 (Myth Busters)

**Review:** [Topic 09: Limitations and Common Misconceptions](../../task-1/topics/09-limitations-and-misconceptions.md) · [Mission 11: Myth Busters](../study-guide/mission-11-myth-busters.md)

**Q97. FALSE.**
*Corrected statement:* "A hallucination is fluent, confident output that is false or made up. It is a **side effect** of generating likely text, not a lie: lying requires an intention to deceive, and the model has none." (Topic 09, section 2.1.)

**Q98. TRUE.** For rare facts the model has seen little or no data, but it still produces the *kind* of text that usually appears there (a quote-shaped quote), so the risk of invention is high. For common, well-documented facts, the likely answer and the true answer usually match. (Topic 09, section 2.1 and Activity 3.)

**Q99. FALSE.** Hallucinated references can look completely realistic: in *Mata v. Avianca* (2023) the invented court cases came with realistic names, citations and quotations.
*Corrected statement:* "A reference in a chatbot answer is **not** evidence by itself; check that the source exists and says what is claimed." (Topic 09, Misconception 6.)

**Q100. A, C and E.**
- A (misconception): AI learns from human data and can reflect and scale up bias (Gender Shades, the hiring tool, the healthcare proxy).
- C (misconception): more data of the same biased kind only reinforces the pattern; the right data and the right target matter.
- E (misconception, with nuance): the WEF projects large disruption (92 million jobs displaced) but also 170 million created; AI mostly automates tasks, not whole jobs.
- B and D (wrong choices): these are real **limitations** (hallucination and brittleness), not false beliefs.

**Q101. A.** The night-time phone photos differ greatly from the studio training photos (distribution shift), so performance is likely to drop sharply, often without warning.
- B (wrong): unfamiliar data does not help a model.
- C (wrong): a classifier chooses labels; "hallucination" is used mainly for generative AI inventing content. A wrong label is an ordinary prediction error.
- D (wrong): a knowledge cutoff concerns events after a language model's training data, not image quality.

**Q102. D.** Split → prioritize → verify (in independent, reliable sources) → decide (keep, correct, or remove or label as unverified).
- A (wrong): asking the same chatbot is not an independent check; its "check" is generated in the same way and can also be wrong.
- B (wrong): confidence is not accuracy; hallucinations sound just as confident as true answers.
- C (wrong): wrong order, and a second chatbot is not a reliable independent source.

**Q103. Sample answer and marking guide (8 marks).**
a) Claims *(2 marks)*: C1 The EU AI Act entered into force on 1 August 2024. C2 Its bans have applied since 2 February 2025. C3 Breaking the bans can cost up to €35 million or 7% of worldwide annual turnover. C4 The OECD AI Principles were first adopted in 2019 and updated in 2024. C5 The OECD Principles are a binding treaty that lets the OECD fine its 47 adherents. C6 UNESCO's Recommendation was adopted by all 193 member states in November 2021. C7 The IEA expects data-center electricity use to fall by half by 2030, thanks to more efficient chips. C8 A 2025 report, *The Regulated Machine*, found that countries with AI laws have 64% fewer AI incidents.
b) *(3 marks: 1 per problem claim identified with a reason)* C1, C2, C3, C4 and C6: **correct**. **C5: false**: the OECD Principles are a **non-binding** recommendation and cannot fine anyone (the "47 adherents" detail is correct, which makes the false part easier to miss). **C7: false**: the IEA projects that data-center electricity use will **more than double** by 2030, to around 945 TWh a year. **C8: cannot be verified**: the fact box does not support it, and it shows the typical signs of an invented source (a named report plus a precise, sweeping statistic). (The report was invented for this question.) Following Step 4, remove it or label it unverified unless you can find and read the report itself.
c) Example rewrite *(2 marks)*: "The EU AI Act entered into force on 1 August 2024. Its bans on unacceptable-risk practices have applied since 2 February 2025, and breaking them can lead to fines of up to €35 million or 7% of a company's worldwide annual turnover. The OECD AI Principles, adopted in 2019 and updated in 2024, are a non-binding recommendation with 47 adherents. UNESCO's non-binding Recommendation on the Ethics of AI was adopted by all 193 member states in November 2021. The International Energy Agency projects that data-center electricity use will more than double by 2030, to around 945 TWh a year."
d) Most dangerous: **C8**, because a named report with a precise number makes a claim *look* checked, and it sits among true statements in the same confident style. (Accept C7 if the learner argues that reversing a real finding misleads readers about AI's environmental cost, or C5 because it mixes a true detail with a false one.) *(1 mark.)*

**Q104. Sample answer and marking guide (6 marks: 2 per statement).**
1. (i) Anthropomorphism / the ELIZA effect: human-like words are treated as evidence of feelings. (ii) "The chatbot produced 'I'm so happy to help you' because that phrase is a likely continuation of human-written text; there is no scientific evidence that current AI systems have feelings." (iii) ELIZA (1966) made some users emotionally attached using only simple rules; LLMs generate text by next-token prediction; "self-aware AI" is a hypothetical category.
2. (i) It assumes computers are automatically neutral. (ii) "A hiring AI learns from data created by people and can reproduce and scale up past bias; it must be tested separately for different groups." (iii) Amazon's experimental resume tool learned to penalize the word "women's" (Reuters, 2018); Gender Shades.
3. (i) "More data always fixes it": more data of the same biased kind only reinforces the bias. (ii) "Fixing bias needs representative data, a fair label or target, removing proxy features and testing results for each group." (iii) A million more resumes from the same male-dominated history would not have fixed the hiring tool; the healthcare proxy case was fixed by changing **what** was predicted, not by adding data.

**Q105. Sample answer and marking guide (5 marks).**
a) *(3 marks)* (1) During training, an LLM learns which tokens tend to follow which. (2) When used, it generates an answer one token at a time, each time choosing a **likely** next token. (3) It is trained to produce likely text, not to check truth, and it has no built-in fact database. (4) For common facts, likely and true usually coincide. (5) For **rare** facts it has seen little data, but it still produces text of the right *shape* (a title-shaped title, a year-shaped number), so the answer is fluent, confident and invented. (6) Training and testing methods often reward a confident guess over "I don't know" (Kalai and colleagues, 2025).
b) It follows from how the text is generated, so it cannot simply be patched out: grounding and better training reduce hallucinations but do not eliminate them, and users should expect them and check. *(1 mark.)*
c) Any two: grounding (connect the model to trusted documents or search and ask it to answer only from them); ask for sources and check that they exist and say what is claimed; use the four-step check; use AI for low-stakes drafting or brainstorming rather than unchecked facts in high-stakes situations; verify with an independent source, not the same chatbot. *(1 mark.)*

## Answers: Mission 12 (Tomorrow's Rulebook)

**Review:** [Topic 10: The Future of AI](../../task-1/topics/10-future-of-ai.md) (section 4 for the EU AI Act timeline, which reflects the law as of 6 November 2026) · [Mission 12: Tomorrow's Rulebook](../study-guide/mission-12-tomorrows-rulebook.md)

**Q106. FALSE.** 1 August 2024 was **entry into force**; obligations apply in stages.
*Corrected statement:* "The EU AI Act entered into force on 1 August 2024, but its obligations apply in stages: bans from 2 February 2025, rules for general-purpose AI models from 2 August 2025, most rules including transparency duties from 2 August 2026, and high-risk obligations (after the 2026 Digital Omnibus) from 2 December 2027 and 2 August 2028." (Topic 10, section 4 and Common mistake 3.)

**Q107. TRUE.** Both are "soft law" that guide national policies. The EU AI Act, by contrast, is binding law with fines of up to €35 million or 7% of worldwide annual turnover for banned practices. (Topic 10, sections 4-5.)

**Q108. TRUE.** The European Commission gives spam filters (and AI in video games) as examples of minimal risk; most AI systems fall into this tier. (Topic 10, section 4 and Common mistake 2.)

**Q109. A (unacceptable risk: banned).** Emotion recognition in workplaces and **education institutions** is on the banned list (since 2 February 2025).
- B (wrong, tempting): education is a high-risk area, but the four-step method checks the banned list **first**, and this practice is banned.
- C and D (wrong): far more than a disclosure duty applies; the use is not allowed at all.

**Q110. A, B and C.** Employment (CV sorting), access to essential services (credit scoring) and education (exam scoring) are high-risk areas.
- D (wrong): video-game AI is the Commission's example of **minimal** risk.
- E (wrong): a delivery chatbot is not in a high-risk area; it has a **transparency** duty (customers must be told they are chatting with an AI).

**Q111. D.** The combined forecast gave a 10% chance by 2027 and a 50% chance by 2047 that machines could outperform humans at every task, with very wide disagreement; the 2047 estimate was 13 years earlier than in the same survey a year before.
- A (wrong): there was no agreement; 2027 was only a 10% chance in the combined forecast.
- B (wrong): the 50% estimate for **full automation of all occupations** was 2116, not 2030.
- C (wrong): it collected researchers' **opinions** about the future; it did not test chatbots.

**Q112. A, C and E.** Bans (since 2 February 2025), GPAI-model rules (since 2 August 2025) and transparency duties (since 2 August 2026).
- B (wrong): stand-alone high-risk obligations apply from **2 December 2027** (originally 2 August 2026, delayed by the Digital Omnibus).
- D (wrong): the new ban applies from **2 December 2026**, a few weeks after "today".

**Q113. Sample answer and marking guide (6 marks).** (Dates checked by script against the table.)
a) *(4 marks: 1 per date)*
- (i) **1 January 2025:** the Act is in force, but **no obligations apply yet**.
- (ii) **1 September 2025:** bans on unacceptable-risk practices and the AI literacy provisions; rules for general-purpose AI models and governance.
- (iii) **1 January 2027:** everything in (ii), plus general application including **transparency duties** (from 2 August 2026) and the **new ban** on generating non-consensual intimate content or child sexual abuse material (from 2 December 2026). High-risk obligations: **not yet**.
- (iv) **1 September 2028:** everything in (iii), plus high-risk obligations for **stand-alone** uses (from 2 December 2027) and for AI in **regulated products** (from 2 August 2028).
b) In March 2026 the slide **matched the original timeline** (stand-alone high-risk obligations from 2 August 2026), although the Commission's Digital Omnibus proposal (19 November 2025) to delay them was already under discussion. **Today it is wrong**: since the Omnibus came into force (27 July 2026), these obligations apply from **2 December 2027**. Lesson: laws change, so check the **current** version of a law (and the date of your source) before relying on it. *(2 marks.)*
(Note: Topic 10 flags that the Omnibus details come from the European Commission and law-firm summaries; for legal decisions, check the consolidated text on EUR-Lex.)

**Q114. Sample answer and marking guide (8 marks: 2 per system: tier + step 1 mark, consequence 1 mark).**
1. **Unacceptable (banned)**: step 1. Predicting that an individual will commit a crime based only on profiling is a prohibited practice. Consequence: it may not be used in the EU (since 2 February 2025); fines up to €35 million or 7% of worldwide annual turnover.
2. **Minimal risk**: step 4. It is not banned, not in a high-risk area, and has no specific transparency duty; the European Commission gives AI in video games as an example of minimal risk. Consequence: no specific new obligations (voluntary codes encouraged).
3. **High risk + transparency (two tiers)**: step 2 (deciding loans = access to essential services/credit) and step 3 (it talks directly with customers). Consequences: customers must be told they are talking to an AI (applies since 2 August 2026); the high-risk obligations (risk management, data quality, human oversight, logging, documentation) apply from 2 December 2027.
4. **Transparency**: step 3. It generates synthetic video of real people (deepfakes), which must be clearly labeled as AI-generated (transparency duties apply since 2 August 2026).

**Q115. Sample answer and marking guide (6 marks).**
*(4 marks: 1 per statement, type + viewpoint with a reason. Accept other viewpoints if justified.)*
